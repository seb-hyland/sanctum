1. What are three reasons for designing drug delivery systems? I.e., why not deliver the drug(s) alone?
- Locality of delivery and means of absorbance
  - You may wish for some drugs to be delivered directly into the patient's bloodstream
    - e.g., intravenous injection
  - Other times, it may be optimal to deliver via digestion or other indirect means
    - e.g., pills, patches, etc.
- Rate of absorbance
  - Some drugs may require very rapid uptake, especially if they provide immediate relief to an issue
    - e.g., Epipens require rapid uptake since they are designed to provide fast, emergency relief to extreme allergic responses
  - In other cases, drugs may be designed with the goal of promoting slower, prolonged uptake
    - e.g., diphenhydramine (brand name Benadryl) has a mean serum elimination half-life of 9.2 +/- 2.5 hours in young adults (Simons et al., 1990). This is likely due to the fact that it is designed as a long-acting antihistamine, and thus is delivered via digestion (delivery method promotes slow uptake).

2. Name two major challenge(s) associated with designing drug delivery systems.
- The toxicity of particles used in drug delivery, especially for novel approaches such as nanoparticle-aided delivery
  - "In vivo and in vitro experimentation has shown the harmful effect of silver, gold, silica, and titanium used as nanoparticles for coupling and delivering drugs" (Sharma et al., 2021)
- Natural biological barriers to drug delivery, such as the blood-brain barrier and intestinal barrier
  - The blood-brain barrier prevents entry of carrier particles into the brain and CNS, which presents a significant barrier toward the treatment of cerebral diseases (Danaei et al., 2018)
